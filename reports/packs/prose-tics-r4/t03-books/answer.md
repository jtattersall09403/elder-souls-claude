PICK: A
CONFIDENCE: high

- A runs a four-beat formula with a fixed refrain — "prayed to the saints", then "[NAME-15] X the Y appeared before [NAME-6] in shining robes / in golden armor / with flesh like sparkling gems / in a burst of flame, and smiled upon him" — four brothers, four saints, four gifts. That repetition-with-variation is how a shipped game writes a hagiography that has to be readable in a 400-word window.
- A treats the bestiary as furniture rather than as description: "rip a trama shrub up by the roots with two fingers", "She had a face like a dreugh". A writer inventing a world for the first time explains those; a writer working inside a shipped one does not.
- A ends on a moral delivered by a fifth saint as a gag: "[NAME-6], before you fight, find out what you're fighting for." The whole tale is a setup for that line, and the reward being worthless — a wife with "a very, very, very unpleasant personality" and an estate "utterly dissolute by decades of blight storms" — is the joke a game tells about quest rewards.
- B is a first-person political testimony with no story in it at all: an argument about how a protectorate was assembled out of small reasonable requests ("It wants water, then it wants a warehouse, then it wants a wall round the warehouse"). It is addressed to an enemy readership and it contains a thesis, not an incident.
- Recorded against my own pick: B is the stronger piece of writing by a wide margin. "It was a purchase from people who did not know they owned anything and could not have sold it if they had" and "his shape lets the [NAME-3] keep its dignity and ours lets us keep something we needed more than dignity" are doing something A never attempts.

WEAKEST POINT: A is damaged in the same way as other passages I have seen ("casting aspersions o one another's looks" in a sibling trial; here "[NAME-6] look a look at his sad, cut-rate iron blade"), and a mangled text may be a processed text.
