Three Imperials have asked me for this. One was a poet and one was a surveyor and one was a decent man from the Office who wanted it for a file. I said no to all three and I am writing it for my own people, in your language, because my own people can read your language and yours cannot read ours, and I would like this to be a thing you have to be told about rather than a thing you can pick up.

Here is what they call the agreements.

A ship comes into a landing. It wants water, then it wants a warehouse, then it wants a wall round the warehouse, then it wants the wall garrisoned, then it wants the road to the wall kept clear. Each of those is a small reasonable request and each is agreed to by whoever is standing at the landing that morning, because refusing a small reasonable request in front of a ship is how you find out what the ship is for. At the end there is a fort, and a paper, and the paper is signed by a person who never in their life thought they were signing for a province, because in this country nobody signs for a province. There is no such person. Your Empire has looked for one for three hundred years and it has settled, every time, for whoever would hold the pen.

They are not wrong that this was not a conquest. It was not. It was a purchase from people who did not know they owned anything and could not have sold it if they had.

About Lilmoth I will say only that Lilmoth was not agreed and everyone knows Lilmoth was not agreed and there is a customs house there anyway, and that the Office's own writers put this in their books, in a small sad voice, as a thing that is regrettable, in the way a man regrets the weather.

Now the ford.

My grandmother's cousin's people held the weirs above the crossing. That is not a story; it is a thing you can go and look at, the posts are still there. The column came down in the dry month because they had been told the dry month was safe, and they had been told that by a survey made by a man who had seen the reach in the dry month once.

The weirs were opened. This is not in dispute among us and I am not going to pretend it is. What is in dispute is what happened next, and the honest answer, which nobody on either side likes, is that opening the weirs put a great deal of water into a channel that was already carrying a great deal of water, and nobody standing at the weirs could have known what the reach would do with it. How many died is given as one hundred and fifty-one in their books and as eleven in ours, and there is a woman at Lilmoth who was closer to it than any of us and who says the loss was the carts. I do not know. Nobody who tells you they know was there.

Your Tribune has written that the weirs could not have drowned a column, and he has an appendix. I have read his appendix. It is arithmetic done on the volume of the weir ponds, and it is correct, and it is arithmetic about the wrong thing: it is not the water in the ponds that kills a man crossing, it is the shoulder of water that goes down the channel when the ponds go at once, and I have watched a small pond do that to a boat.

So: he says a flood. We say a woman. The truth appears to be a woman who caused a flood and did not know she could, and both sides have arranged that story into the shape they wanted, and his shape lets the Empire keep its dignity and ours lets us keep something we needed more than dignity.

And here is the part he half sees and cannot say.

He writes that no column has gone inland in thirty-six years and that there is no order to that effect. That is true. He thinks this is a curious administrative fact. It is the whole settlement. Your Empire holds the coast because we let it hold the coast, and it does not go inland because it found out once what inland is, and both of us have agreed to call that arrangement a province so that neither of us has to do anything about it.

We are not free. I want to be very clear, because there are young ones who read this sort of thing and get an idea. They take our people north out of Blackrose under a lease that your own Assize signs. They take them off the coast entirely in ships out of Tear and your law says that is nothing to do with your law. A people who are not invaded and are still carried off in boats are not a people who have won anything.

What we have is a border that is not on any of their maps, and it runs about two days inland, and it holds because of a woman with a sluice gate and a column of men who were told the dry month was safe.
